Deep RL for Smart Home Energy Management

Comparing perfect-foresight MPC, a rule-based controller, and a TD3 agent across four building/climate scenarios.

PythonPyTorchDeep RLTD3Model Predictive ControlPuLP / GurobiGymnasium

Overview

A Python SHEMS simulator that compares three controllers, perfect-foresight Model Predictive Control (MPC), a fixed-threshold rule-based controller, and a Twin-Delayed DDPG (TD3) deep reinforcement learning agent, on a building with an independent HVAC unit and water heater. Grew out of a Data Science Research Intern role at Green Energy Park (Ben Guerir, Morocco), where a residential PV/battery/HVAC system was first modeled as a Mixed-Integer Linear Program, then extended into this four-scenario comparative study across France and Morocco, plus a regulatory case study.

Problem

Coordinating HVAC, water heating, battery storage, and solar generation hour by hour to minimize cost while respecting occupant comfort is a sequential decision problem. Model-based optimization (MPC) needs a forecast; model-free reinforcement learning doesn't, but has to learn the tradeoff from experience. Whether that comparison holds across climates, building types, and real market regulations was the open question.

Approach

  1. Formalize the control problem as an MDPThree endogenous state variables (battery state of charge, indoor air temperature, water-heater energy), a 3-dimensional continuous action expressed as a target fill level for each store (not a raw power flow, the design choice that stabilizes DRL training here), and an hourly reward of profit minus a soft comfort penalty.
  2. Build one shared physics engineA single dispatch_step function implements the battery, HVAC (RC-node thermal model), and water-heater transition equations once, and is called identically by all three controllers, so differences in results come from the policy, not from three different simulators.
  3. Implement the perfect-foresight upper boundSolve a mixed-integer linear program per calendar month with PuLP/CBC, chaining state across months, then replay every solved decision through the shared physics engine to confirm the LP's reported objective matches the simulated reward.
  4. Implement the realistic baselineA fixed-threshold rule-based controller: always request max battery charge/discharge, recharge the water heater below 70% of its range, and apply a symmetric dead-band to the bidirectional HVAC (heat below 30% of the comfort band, cool above 70%, coast in between).
  5. Train the learned controllerTwin-Delayed DDPG (TD3): clipped double-Q critics, target policy smoothing, and delayed policy updates on top of an actor-critic MLP (hidden sizes 300, 600), trained for 3,001 episodes of 24 simulated hours each.
  6. Run the comparisonEvaluate all three controllers on held-out test days across four building/climate scenarios (commercial and residential, in temperate Lyon and hot/arid Ben Guerir), then run a fifth regulatory case study modeling a running monthly net-metering export cap.

MPC is the clear profit upper bound in every scenario and, because it can plan comfort-band excursions around future PV, keeps its comfort penalty an order of magnitude below the other two controllers. The purely reactive rule-based controller incurs by far the largest comfort penalty (24–70 EUR/day). TD3 sits between the two: it cuts the comfort penalty by 65–96% relative to rule-based in every scenario, and beats rule-based on total reward in all four scenarios, at a small, scenario-dependent cost in raw profit.

Regulatory case study — a running monthly net-metering export cap (grid export beyond 20% of the month's cumulative PV is billed at the buy price, not credited at the sell price) disproportionately penalizes foresight-less control:

Mechanism-level finding: replaying one representative month shows MPC crossing into the penalized export tier for just 1.14 kWh out of 80.38 kWh exported, versus 9.14 kWh out of 85.57 kWh for the rule-based controller, roughly 8× more over-cap exposure despite exporting only marginally more. A running cap rewards the ability to anticipate the month's remaining PV, not just to conserve energy today. Giving TD3 two extra forecast-derived features (cap headroom, rest-of-month PV forecast) let it beat rule-based on reward in both regulated scenarios, though neither closed the gap to MPC's month-long perfect foresight.

MPC also achieves the highest self-sufficiency in every scenario (up to 74.6% in Ben Guerir residential), and every controller loses less money in Ben Guerir than in Lyon, more PV yield and a smaller net heating load, with the residential building benefiting proportionally more from the climate switch (MPC profit improves 81% residential vs. 32% commercial).
