Industrial Production Optimization

A Gurobi LP/MILP production plan that cuts weekly cost by up to 28% against a conventional baseline.

PythonGurobiOperations ResearchLinear ProgrammingMILP

Overview

An optimization pipeline for industrial production planning across 10 products, 6 machines, and a 12-week horizon, formulated as a linear program and a mixed-integer extension, both solved with Gurobi and benchmarked against a conventional planning baseline.

Problem

Production planning, how much of each product to make, on which machine, and when, is naturally a constrained optimization problem, but ad hoc or heuristic planning leaves real savings on the table once machine capacity, changeover costs, and material availability all interact.

Approach

  1. Forecast demandFeed a machine-learning demand forecast (built in a dedicated notebook) into the planning model as the weekly demand parameter for each of 10 products over a 12-week horizon.
  2. Formulate Model A (LP)A continuous linear program: production quantity, ending inventory, shortage, and overtime-hours variables per product/machine/week, subject to demand balance, machine-hour capacity, and raw-material availability across 13 materials and 20 valid product/machine routings.
  3. Extend to Model B (MILP)Add binary setup/allocation decisions, setup time, setup cost, and a minimum batch size per product/machine pair, the realistic version of the plan that accounts for changeover overhead the LP ignores.
  4. Benchmark against a conventional baselineCompare both optimized plans' total cost against a conventional (non-optimized) planning heuristic on the same demand and capacity data.
  5. Stress-test with scenario & sensitivity analysisRe-solve the model under demand surges (+10/20/30%), a simulated machine failure, and reduced shortage-cost assumptions, to see how the plan and its cost degrade under pressure.

Results

ModelTotal costvs. baseline
Baseline (conventional planning)$100,978.64—
Model A (LP, no setup)$72,878.47−27.8%
Model B (MILP, with setup)$84,286.66−16.5%

Model B costs more than Model A because it accounts for real changeover setup time and cost, machine-hour capacity Model A implicitly ignores, so its 16.5% savings is the more realistic figure for a deployable plan, while Model A's 27.8% is the theoretical ceiling with unlimited free changeovers.

Cost degrades non-linearly with demand: a 30% demand surge more than doubles shortage cost relative to the base case (from $1,218 to $18,124), while a single machine failure barely moves total cost, evidence the plan already carries enough slack capacity to absorb one equipment loss but not a sustained demand shock.
